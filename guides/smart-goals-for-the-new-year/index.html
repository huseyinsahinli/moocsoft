SMART Goals for the New Year: 3 Worked Examples

Imagine opening a library book on the first Monday after the holidays. You have forty minutes before the bus. Your goal says “learn more this year,” and twelve of those minutes disappear while you choose a chapter. Change the instruction to “answer the three questions on page forty and mark the one I can't explain.” You can begin while your coat is still on the chair.

The useful takeaway

Write a goal you can try in the next seven days, then decide what evidence you want to have in hand at the review.

Give SMART goals for the new year a real appointment

SMART goals for the new year work best when somebody else could read your sentence and tell what you plan to do. Start with an action you control, an occasion to do it and a date to inspect the result. Then test it against the time you actually have. A beautifully precise goal for a free hour you never get is still a bad appointment.

Give each letter a question

Specific asks what you'll do. Measurable asks what evidence the action will leave. Achievable asks whether you have the time, access and money it requires. Relevant asks which part of your life it improves. Time-bound asks when you'll take stock. You can answer all five in two sentences. If choosing a direction is the harder part, use the 2027 resolution planning worksheet first.

Write a baseline before you pick a number

Look at a normal week. Say you want to read twenty books this year. How much did you read last Tuesday? If it was six pages on the train, six pages tells you more about your next appointment than twenty books does. Choose one train ride next week, put a book in your bag, and see how many pages you enjoy before your stop. That observation gives you a starting point.

A baseline can be a sample rather than a formal test. For a work presentation, record yourself explaining one slide. Listen once. Maybe the explanation takes ninety seconds because you keep looking for the example you meant to give. Your practice goal could be to explain that example clearly using one index card. An arbitrary target to speak faster would leave the actual difficulty untouched.

Before increasing a number, ask what reaching it would cost. Forty pages a week could fit four train rides, or it could consume the one quiet evening you share with your partner. Both schedules reach forty. Only you can decide which one belongs on your calendar. Write the cost beside the target while you can still change either of them.

Three worked examples you can borrow

The student who wants to understand statistics

Try this version for two weeks: “After my Tuesday and Thursday lectures, I'll answer two practice questions without looking at my notes. I'll check each answer and bring one confusing step to the next class.” The measurable part is four attempted questions per week. Getting them all right would be welcome, but a wrong answer with a well-marked sticking point still does the job.

At the review, lay out the eight attempts. If the same error appears three times, spend the next session asking for help with that step. If you completed every question with ease, choose a harder set. The target has produced evidence about what to learn next, which is much more valuable than eight tidy ticks on a page.

The employee who wants to go home on time

“Leave earlier” is tricky when a customer can call at closing time. Try “At 4:35 on my three office days, I'll write tomorrow's first task, save the document I'm editing and flag anything that needs a handover.” Set the first review for the end of two working weeks. Record when you finished and what happened on the days you stayed late.

You might discover that the difficult days all involve a meeting scheduled until five. A conversation about that meeting is a sensible next action. Working twice as fast through lunch would be a poor answer to a calendar problem. Keep the handover if it helps you and use the record to explain the change you need from somebody else.

The household putting money aside for an August trip

Suppose you want to set aside $360 more and have twelve paydays available before you must pay. A starting figure is $30 per payday. Look at the next two paydays individually before choosing it. A school fee may make $10 realistic on the first date, leaving $350 across eleven. If those later amounts look tight, change the trip budget while you still have choices. Decide what you'll count on a difficult day

Consider a piano player who wants to practise for twenty minutes three times a week. During the first session she spends seventeen minutes playing familiar pieces because the difficult passage is on the next page. The timer gives full credit. Her fingers still hesitate at the same change of chord. A more useful measure is to start at that bar, play it slowly three times, then try it inside the phrase.

Decide beforehand how a shortened attempt will appear in your notes. A five-minute session can be labelled “short, chord change only.” At the weekly review you can see whether those five minutes helped. If you give every attempt the same mark, you'll have to reconstruct what happened from memory. If you discard the short attempts, you'll lose information about what fits on workdays.

For a movement goal, the simplest evidence might be the route you took and whether you had time to eat afterward. Try one of these fifteen health and fitness resolutions for ordinary weeks if you're stuck for a starting point. Choose a measure you can capture in the place where the activity ends. A pencil by the piano beats a spreadsheet you only open at the office.

Run a two-week trial and read the evidence

Write the review appointment when you write the goal. Give it a modest job: decide to keep, change or retire one part of the plan. For the library example, bring your marked questions. For the office example, bring the six departure notes. Reading an actual Tuesday entry takes less effort than trying to remember how the last fortnight felt.

Divide a piece of paper into two columns. On the left, copy your promise exactly as you wrote it. On the right, describe what happened on the most awkward occasion. “Needed headphones and had left them at home” suggests a different repair from “Did the exercise and couldn't tell whether my answer was right.” Make the change close to the obstacle. Put spare headphones in the bag, or choose a practice book with an answer key.

A useful deadline also gives you permission to stop measuring. Put a line beneath the last entry and write one sentence about what to carry forward. If the question that matters has changed, let the next fortnight answer the new question instead of collecting another page of figures you already understand. The CFPB includes a practical SMART goals worksheet in its free toolkit.

One last test for SMART goals for the new year: read yours aloud as though you're handing a friend the instructions for tomorrow. If your friend asks “Which book?” or “Before or after class?”, fill in the missing detail. A usable sentence gives you somewhere to start even after you've forgotten how inspired you felt when you wrote it.

Common questions

Does every SMART goal need a percentage?

A count, date or finished object can be easier to check. A recorded two-minute rehearsal gives you something concrete to review. A promise to become twenty percent more confident leaves you guessing what to measure.

Can I set a SMART goal with someone else?

Agree on the part each person controls and put both names beside their actions. For a shared meal plan, one person might choose two recipes on Thursday and the other check the cupboard before Friday's shop.

What if the first target was too easy?

Look at what you wanted it to improve. If the action already solves the problem, keep its size. If you're ready for a harder task, change one feature for the next trial so you can tell whether the change helped.
